Research note for review.

Executive summary

The answer: Inferred: Do not rebuild the programme as described; test it first, because the price is fourfold design and maintenance effort, while the quoted cash costs remain unverified.

Point 1: Sourced: The broad evidence does not support organisation-wide adoption. The recent meta-analysis found a small average benefit, but the required pattern across styles was uncommon. Strongest evidence: Only 26% of learning measures favoured matching for at least two styles [2] [frontiersin.org]

Point 2: Sourced: Earlier review evidence and a substantial accessible trial found no dependable matching advantage. Strongest evidence: A review analysed 22 post-test effects and found visual and auditory estimates small and not statistically clear [1] [frontiersin.org]

Point 3: Sourced: A narrow positive result makes a local test reasonable, not a rebuild. Strongest evidence: A randomised study of 42 learners found a comprehension benefit, but no recall benefit [3] [frontiersin.org]

This works if:

  • Clear preferences exist and matching improves delayed learning across different preferences.
  • Formats contain equal content, practice and time.
  • Learning gains justify the extra creation and maintenance work.

It fails if:

  • Matching improves liking but not delayed retention or workplace application.
  • One generally stronger format explains the result.
  • Leaders cannot be placed clearly into the four categories.

One-month test

Who takes part: Inferred: Recruit 32 leaders, aiming for eight who clearly state each preference. Record preference with one neutral question on paper or an ordinary spreadsheet. Use no profile, scoring system or vendor content.

What they do: Inferred: Build two 20-minute lessons from existing material. Give each identical objectives, facts and practice, delivered as visual, auditory, reading/writing or kinaesthetic. Randomly give every leader one matched lesson and one mismatched lesson. Reverse topic and order across participants. The assumption is that “four versions” means delivery differs while content and difficulty stay equal.

What we measure: Inferred: Use the same immediate quiz, a three-week delayed quiz and one workplace scenario scored without knowing the assigned version. Record completion time and enjoyment separately.

Stop rule: Inferred: Stop the proposal if any condition applies:

  • Matched delivery fails to beat mismatched delivery by 10 percentage points on both delayed measures.
  • Any preference group does worse when matched.
  • Only enjoyment improves.
  • Too few leaders have a clear single preference.

Pitch

Decision sought: Inferred: Approve a bounded internal test, not a programme redesign or vendor purchase.

Portfolio case: Inferred: The question is whether personalisation lifts leadership learning enough to justify added complexity. The test measures retained knowledge and application, not enthusiasm alone.

Budget case: Inferred: Existing content, internal participants and a one-question preference record keep external spend at zero. Tracking build hours will expose the likely operating burden.

Guardrail: Inferred: A tie, mixed result or satisfaction-only gain stops the learning-style model. A result that clears the stop rule would justify a larger test, not immediate rollout.

Where both sides are right

  • Claim 1: Matching can improve comprehension when a learner has a clear preference and the task resembles the small study. It does not support four programme versions when benefits fail to appear across styles.
  • Claim 2: Matching can reduce effort when the preferred format also supports effective learning strategies. It fails as a learning rule when comfort does not improve delayed retention or application.
  • Claim 3: Both sides assume the annual profile estimate reflects a real purchase option. If that is not true, the business case needs a new cost baseline.
  • Claim 4: Both sides assume the quoted development estimate reflects equal-quality versions. If that is not true, the implementation trade-off remains unknown.
  • Claim 5: Both sides assume the reported Harvard study exists and concerns relevant managers. If that is not true, the proposal loses its headline support.
  • Claim 6: Both sides assume leaders’ stated preferences predict learning outcomes. If that is not true, preference and satisfaction cannot justify matching.
  • Claim 7: Both sides assume the present programme is uniform enough for the proposed model to be a meaningful contrast. If that is not true, the test must first define current variation.
  • Claim 8: Both sides assume organisation size changes the validity of the core learning claim. If that is not true, size affects scale and cost, not whether matching works.

Words used in this note

  • Matching hypothesis: the claim that people learn better when teaching format matches their stated preference.
  • Crossover interaction: evidence that one format works best for one preference while another format works best for a different preference.
  • Delayed retention: what a learner still remembers after time has passed.
  • Meta-analysis: a statistical combination of results from several studies.
  • Multimodal instruction: giving the same learners material through more than one mode.
  • Randomised crossover test: a test in which assignment and order vary, and each participant experiences more than one condition.
  • Workplace application: using what was learned in a realistic management situation.
